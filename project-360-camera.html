Seeing the wholeclassroom.

A camera at the center of the group's table, instead of the corner of the room, quadrupled how often we could detect students' faces. Better placement beat fancier algorithms.

With: Chris Palaguachi and Dr. Jina Kang · Published: Journal of Educational Data Mining (2025); short-paper version won an honorable mention at EDM 2024.

The problem

If you want to understand how students collaborate, you need to see them: faces for engagement, posture for attention, gestures for participation. Researchers usually mount ordinary cameras on classroom walls, and those cameras have a terrible view. Students sit with their backs turned, lean over worksheets, and block each other constantly. When face detection fails, everything built on top of it fails silently, and the analytics quietly describe only the students the camera happened to see.

What I did

We placed a 360-degree camera at the center of each group's table, where every student faces inward toward it, and recorded the same collaborative sessions with traditional cameras at the same time. Then we ran identical facial-recognition and pose-estimation pipelines on both recordings and compared what each camera could actually capture, student by student, moment by moment.

What we found

The center-of-table 360 camera quadrupled the facial-detection rate in group settings. The gain came from geometry, not algorithms: a camera in the middle of a circle of people simply sees faces that a wall camera never will. Pose estimation showed trade-offs in both directions, which the paper maps out in detail.

Why it matters

In multimodal learning analytics, everyone reaches for a better model. This project is a reminder that data quality is upstream of every model: a $300 hardware decision produced a bigger improvement than any algorithmic tweak we could have made. If your detection rate is low, your first question should be where the camera sits, not which network you fine-tune.
