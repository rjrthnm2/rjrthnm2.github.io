A small modellearns to teach.

I fine-tuned a small language model to stand in for a GPT-4o tutor inside an immersive astronomy simulation. It matched the big model 85% of the time, and failed in ways worth knowing about.

Where: Immersive Data Lab, UIUC · PI: Dr. Jina Kang · Context: the HoloOrbits augmented-reality astronomy simulation.

The problem

AI tutors in classrooms usually mean cloud APIs: every student question travels to a frontier model and back. That brings three problems schools care about deeply. Cost scales with every interaction. Latency breaks the flow of a live lesson. And student data, often from minors, leaves the building. A small language model that runs locally would solve all three, if it can actually teach.

What I did

HoloOrbits already had a working tutor agent powered by GPT-4o. I used that agent to generate synthetic training data, then fine-tuned Microsoft's Phi-3, a model small enough to run on modest hardware, to replicate the tutor's behavior. The evaluation compared the small model's responses against the GPT-4o reference across the tutoring scenarios the simulation covers.

What we found

The fine-tuned Phi-3 replicated 85% of the reference tutor's results. Just as important, its failures were not random noise: the model hallucinated in systematic, identifiable patterns. That distinction matters, because systematic failures can be anticipated, guarded against, and engineered around; random ones cannot.

Why it matters

Small models are a genuine path to affordable, private, low-latency AI in classrooms, but the interesting finding is about evaluation. Reporting a single replication score would have hidden the most useful result: knowing exactly how a model fails is what makes it deployable. If you evaluate an educational model, hunt for the failure structure, not just the success rate.
